The kid who sold a video game at twelve

Pretoria, 1971

Elon Musk was born in Pretoria, South Africa, to an engineer father and a Canadian model-and-nutritionist mother. He was an obsessive reader as a kid, and by age twelve he'd taught himself enough programming to write and sell a simple video game called Blastar.

At seventeen he left South Africa for Canada, eventually landing at the University of Pennsylvania to study physics and economics. Rather than go to graduate school, he rode the early internet boom instead — co-founding Zip2, then the online payments company that became PayPal, both sold for serious money by the time he was in his early thirties.

What followed was a run of increasingly audacious bets: a rocket company built from scratch, an electric car maker he steered from near-collapse to global scale, and now a push into brain interfaces, tunnels, and artificial intelligence — always circling back to the same instinct to go build the thing himself.

Works from first principles

Rather than trust "that's how it's always been done," he tends to break problems down to basic physics and rebuild the answer from there.

Treats failure as data

SpaceX blew up several rockets on the way to orbit; each failure got studied, fixed, and folded into the next attempt.

Stays in the factory, not just the boardroom

He's known for sleeping at the Tesla and SpaceX factories during crunch periods, working engineering problems alongside the floor teams.

Why I admire him

What draws me to Elon Musk isn't just the scale of what he's built — it's how willing he's been to put his own money and reputation behind ideas most experts thought were impossible.

Betting the proceeds from selling PayPal on a rocket company, in an industry no new entrant had cracked in decades, took a kind of conviction that's rare in business. He didn't wait for the technology to be ready; he built teams that made it ready, blown-up rockets and all.

As someone building my own small projects, his story is a reminder that the biggest ideas rarely look sensible from the outside — they start as a stubborn bet by someone willing to absorb the failures along the way.

— with admiration, from one builder to another
